Compounding, explained.

Why money that earns money grows slowly, then suddenly. A placeholder topic that shows off every part of the kit.

⏱ 8 min read🎧 ~6 min audio6 sources
The short version

Four things worth knowing

  1. FactCompounding means your returns earn returns. Growth is a percentage of a growing base. S1
  2. FactAt 7% a year, money doubles in about 10 years; at 10%, in about 7. S2
  3. EstimateOver 30 years, most of the final balance tends to be growth, not deposits. Try it in the chart below.
  4. OpinionTime in the market usually matters more than picking the perfect rate.
First principles

From one euro to many

Tap the section, press →, or use the button to build the idea one step at a time.

1 · You start with a pile

Call it P. This year it earns a rate r, so it grows to P(1 + r). S1

2 · Next year, the interest earns too

Year two applies r to the bigger pile, giving P(1 + r)². The extra bit is interest on interest.

3 · Repeat n times

The curve bends upward. Early years look flat; later years do most of the work.
A = P (1 + r)n

What the formula leaves out

2–3%

Typical yearly inflation eats into real growth. S4

0.2%

A low index-fund fee, versus 1–2% for many active funds. S5

−34%

A single bad year can happen. Averages hide the path. S6

Key ideaCompounding rewards time more than anything else you control.
Watch outFees compound too. A 1% fee over 30 years can cost roughly a quarter of the final balance.
TipAutomate monthly contributions so they happen without a decision.
Example€200 a month for 30 years at 7% grows to about €244k, from €72k paid in.

Show the maths for monthly deposits

FV = P(1+i)n + D · ((1+i)n − 1) / i

i is the monthly rate, n the number of months, D the monthly deposit.
