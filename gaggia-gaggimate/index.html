Inside the Gaggia Classic Pro, wire by wire.

How the E24 turns tap water into espresso, what every thermostat, switch and valve does, and what the GaggiMate kit rewires so a computer, not a bimetal disc, decides the temperature and pressure.

⏱ 18 min read🎧 ~14 min audio23 sources
The short version

Three things worth knowing

  1. FactThe stock machine is three switches and three heat-sensitive switches. Two thermostats (brew 107 °C, steam 145 °C) click the heater on and off, a 184 °C thermal fuse is the last line of defence, and the brew switch powers the pump and the three-way valve together. S4 S8
  2. FactGaggiMate swaps the thermostat for a sensor plus a brain. The brew thermostat is replaced by a thermocouple, the heater is driven through an SSR, and the board gets its own wires to the pump and the valve, so it can run each one separately. Pro adds a pressure sensor. S1 S2
  3. OpinionThe real upgrade is a feedback loop. Steadier temperature, preinfusion, pressure and flow profiles and stop-by-weight all come from the same idea: measure, compare to a target, correct. That turns dialling in from guessing into a controlled experiment.
Mains voltageEverything inside this machine except the GaggiMate low-voltage lines is at 230 V and can kill. This page explains what the kit changes and why. It is not a replacement for the official guide, which you should follow step by step if you ever do it. S1
First principles

A shot is just three problems

Tap the section or press → to build it up.

1 · Move water

Something has to push about 40 g of water through a packed bed of coffee. That is the pump's job.

2 · Heat it to the right temperature

Water near 90–96 °C dissolves the flavours we want quickly. Too cool tastes sour, too hot can taste harsh. The boiler does this. S20

3 · Control how it meets the coffee

The puck pushes back. Pressure only exists because the coffee has resistance. Without a puck, the same pump just sprays water at near-zero pressure.
flow ≈ pressure ÷ resistance
Why this mattersGrind finer and resistance rises. At the same pump effort, flow drops and pressure at the puck rises toward the limit the valve allows. Your grinder setting is literally a resistance knob. The espresso briefing covers how this links to extraction yield.
Gentle mathsFact EY = TDS × drink mass ÷ dose. 18 g in, 36 g out, 10% TDS → 0.10 × 36 ÷ 18 = 20%. Everything the machine does is about making that number repeatable. S20

    The stock machine · water

    Follow a drop of water

    Pick a mode to see which path is live.

    1. FactTank → pump. A vibratory pump (Ulka EP5 type on 230 V machines) is rated 15 bar. That is what it can push against a closed outlet, not what reaches your coffee. S5 S8
    2. FactThe OPV on the pump outlet is a fixed spring valve. Once pressure passes its set point, it sends surplus water back to the tank. Sources disagree on the factory figure: the 2019 service manual says 11 bar (+1/−0.5), retailers say 9–12 bar depending on market, and no measured reading for an EU E24 was found. S5 S6
    3. FactBoiler. On the E24 it is brass (earlier Classic Pros used aluminium). Cold water in, hot water out the top. S7
    4. FactThree-way valve. Powered, it connects boiler to group. Unpowered, it connects the group to the drip tray, which is why you hear a pssht and get a drier puck when you stop the shot. S4 S15
    5. FactSteam. Opening the knob lets steam (or, with the pump running, hot water) leave through the wand. There is no electrical "steaming" state. S4
    The stock machine · electricity

    Six states, no computer

    The stock Gaggia has no microchip deciding anything about the brew. Comori Coffee, an electrical engineer, redrew the official diagram into a functional one. Here are its states. S4

    1. Heat-up: both thermostats closed, heater full on, lamps dark (shorted out).
    2. Brew ready: brew thermostat opens at temperature; current must pass through the brew lamp, which has far higher resistance, so the lamp lights and the heater effectively stops.
    3. Brewing: brew switch powers the pump and the 3-way valve together. Heater still cycles on the brew thermostat.
    4. Steam heat: steam switch bypasses the brew thermostat; heater runs until the steam thermostat opens. It also cuts the valve, so the group stays closed.
    5. Steam ready: steam thermostat opens, steam lamp lights, heater effectively off. It cycles.

    Systems view

    Why stock falls short for control

    A thermostat is a bang-bang controller: it is either fully on or fully off, and it flips at slightly different temperatures going up and coming down. That gap is hysteresis. S19 S17

    • FactTemperature: the heater is either full power or off, with the switch on the outside of the boiler. Where in the cycle you start the shot changes the water temperature. That is why "temperature surfing" exists. S4
    • FactPressure: no gauge, no sensor, a fixed OPV. You cannot see or change it without opening the machine. S6
    • FactPump and valve are welded together by one switch. You cannot have the valve open with the pump paused, which is what a soak-style preinfusion needs. S4
    • OpinionNo stopping by weight. You watch a scale and react, adding a second or two of human lag every shot.

    The intimate part

    What every wiring change does

    For each change: what the part did stock, what is cut or bridged, and what the board gains. Labels (BT, ST, S.x, C.x, PMP) are the official guide's.

    ① Brew thermostat → red jumper + thermocouple

    Stock: BT opens at about 107 °C and stops the heater. Change: unscrew BT, bridge its two wires BT.A–BT.B with a red jumper, screw an M4 thermocouple into the same hole (hand-tight, red = +, blue = −). Gain: the board now reads temperature instead of a disc snapping at one point. S1

    ② Steam thermostat wires → SSR output

    Stock: ST gates the heater. Change: ST.A and ST.B come off the thermostat and are extended with yellow 18 AWG wires to the SSR's output side (polarity doesn't matter on AC). The SSR's input gets the yellow (+) / black (−) signal from the board. The ST can stay in place or be removed. Gain: the board switches the heater's mains current with a few volts, silently and as often as it wants. S1

    ③ Steam switch: S.5 to Neutral, S.1 out, S.4 moved

    Change: S.5 (white) goes to the board's N terminal via blue wire. On 240 V, S.1 is removed and insulated; on both, S.4 (black) moves to the S.1 position. Gain: the board gets neutral for its own supply, and the steam switch no longer bypasses anything at mains level. My reading of the diagram, not stated in the guide. S1

    ④ Brew switch: Eco PCB bypassed, C.4 to Line

    Change (240 V only): C.1 and C.2 come off the switch and are joined with a yellow jumper, taking the Eco board's auto-off out of the loop. Both: C.4 (brown) is extended with grey wire to the board's L terminal. Gain: the board gets switched line power, and standby is now handled by GaggiMate. S1

    ⑤ Pump and valve get separate wires

    Stock: one switch feeds both. Change: PMP.B (grey, not the thermal-fuse side) is lifted off the pump and run by red wire to the board's V (valve) terminal. A new green wire goes from that pump terminal to the board's P (pump) terminal. Gain: pump and valve are independent. Valve open, pump off = soak. Pump at partial power = gentle fill. S1

    ⑥ Pro only: a T in the water line + pressure transducer

    Change: unscrew the pump from the OPV, add an L-fitting and T-fitting, run PTFE tube from the T to a pressure transducer lying at the bottom of the machine, bleed the air out. Gain: the board can see pressure, so it can hold a target pressure or estimate flow and change pump power to match: a closed loop. S1 S3

    ⑦ Optional: keep the physical buttons as low-voltage inputs

    A green/black 4-wire cable connects the free sides of the brew and steam switches (C.1, C.2, S.4, S.5) to the board as sense lines. The guide warns: never mix high and low voltage on the same side of a switch. S1

    First power-on check (from the guide)Heater should not heat at power-on and nothing should run; the SSR's LED tells you if the heater is being driven. Before that: multimeter check for shorts and HV/LV mix-ups, and continuity through the switches. S1
    Physics & electronics

    The five ideas the kit runs on

    PID: a thermostat that does maths

    Fact Output = P (how far off now) + I (how long you've been off) + D (how fast you're changing). Near target it eases off, so it doesn't overshoot like an on/off switch. S11

    Duty cycle: how a switch makes "half power"

    Fact An SSR is still on/off, but switched quickly. On 30% of the time → a 1200 W heater averages 360 W. The PID picks the percentage. S18 S5

    SSR: a switch with no moving parts

    Fact A few volts light an internal LED, which triggers semiconductors that pass mains current. Silent, no contact wear. S12

    Thermocouple: heat makes a voltage

    Fact Two different metals joined at a tip make a tiny voltage that grows with temperature difference: the Seebeck effect. Microvolts per degree, so the board needs an amplifier, and polarity matters. S13 S14

    Solenoid and transducer

    Fact A solenoid is a coil that becomes a magnet and pulls a plunger, opening the valve. A pressure transducer flexes a diaphragm and turns that strain into a voltage. S15 S16

    Not confirmed Exactly how the board varies pump power (e.g. which AC switching method) wasn't stated in the docs I opened; see the GaggiMate source code for that. S23

    So what?

    How this makes coffee better

    1. FactStable temperature. PID control replaces the on/off cycle, so the brew starts at the same temperature each time. S2 S11
    2. FactPhases. A profile is a list of phases (fill, preinfusion, brew); each runs the pump on/off or at a set pressure (Pro), with the valve open or shut, until a weight target or a time limit. The docs' example: 3 s fill, 5 s preinfusion, brew to 36 g at 93 °C. S3
    3. OpinionEven wetting, less channeling. A gentle fill and soak lets the puck swell before full pressure, which most guides credit with fewer weak spots. It's a widely held view; the effect size varies. S20
    4. FactStop by weight. With a Bluetooth scale, the phase ends at the target grams, so the ratio (and therefore EY) stops depending on your reaction time. Without a scale it estimates weight from the pressure curve. S3
    5. OpinionShot diagrams close your loop. Seeing the pressure and weight curves makes the grinder adjustment on the C3 Pro a measured decision rather than a hunch.
    Safety

    Before anyone opens the case

    From the official guide
    • Unplug. Work dry. A boiler stays hot and pressurised after switch-off; wait until it's cold. S1 S6
    • Never mix high voltage (230 V) and low voltage (board signals) on the same side of a switch.
    • Insulate every joint and spare end (heatshrink or tape). Reconnect both grounding wires on the cup warmer.
    • Multimeter check for shorts and continuity before first power-on.
    • Follow local electrical codes. If unsure, use a qualified technician. Modifying the machine voids the warranty. S6
    • Never bridge a blown thermal fuse. S7

    Think about it

    Questions to chew on

    1. Safety lens (failure modes): once the brew thermostat is jumpered out, what still stops the boiler running away if the board or SSR fails "on"? Think about the steam thermostat if you leave it in, and the thermal fuse.
    2. Control lens (decoupling): why does wiring the pump and valve separately make preinfusion possible without buying a fancier pump?
    3. Systems lens (where's the sensor?): the thermocouple sits in the boiler wall, not in the puck. What does that mean for how close "93 °C on screen" is to the water hitting your coffee?
    4. First-principles lens: if pressure only exists because the puck resists, what does a "9 bar profile" do on a puck ground far too coarse?
    Optional practice

    Two back-of-envelope sums

    1 · Heat a cup's worth of waterHeating water takes about 4.18 J per gram per °C. S21 Warming 100 g from 20 °C to 93 °C: 100 × 4.18 × 73 ≈ 30.5 kJ. At 1200 W: 30,500 J ÷ 1200 W ≈ 25 s. Estimate The real warm-up is much longer because the brass boiler and group must heat too, and that metal mass is the reason stable temperature needs patience.
    2 · Duty cycleThe boiler loses heat to the room. Suppose (what-if) it needs 250 W on average to hold temperature. With a 1200 W heater, the PID should settle at about 250 ÷ 1200 ≈ 21% on-time. A thermostat delivers that same average as long full-power bursts instead, which is where the swing comes from.
